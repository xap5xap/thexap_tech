A software idea can feel clear because you can picture the finished product. Try writing the problem before listing features.

A one-page opportunity brief can start with five questions:

1. Who is trying to make progress, and in what situation?
2. What do they do today, including the workaround?
3. What change could the product directly make possible?
4. What will it deliberately leave out?
5. Which assumption would make you reconsider, and what result would lead you to continue or revise?

For a fictional shared request tracker, a modest intended change is to show a recorded owner, status, and next action for each accepted request. That is within the proposed product's scope. Promising that no request will ever be missed would depend on how people use it.

You can start with six lines: user, situation, workaround, intended change, non-goals, and the assumption that determines the next step. Ask someone new to read them. Can they explain the problem and the next decision in a minute?

A clear brief makes the direction reviewable. It does not prove demand, usability, feasibility, or commercial value.
